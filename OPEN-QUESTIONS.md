## Theoretical minimum viable RSI configuration

**Status:** open. No tripwire, no threshold, no answer proposed.

**Why it's here rather than in the main document:** it bears directly on one of the document's central arguments — the physical-friction chokepoint — but nothing below is established fact, and the document's own ceiling and sourcing standards don't accommodate open-ended theoretical exploration at this length. A one-line pointer sits in the document itself; the full treatment is here.

### The premise

The ablation route and the retrospective minimum below are posed entirely from *inside* a hypothetical post-RSI world. RSI has already happened; the loop is already closed and running, continually producing more capable versions of itself (or of the individuals using it), the way the document's own "human-less RSI" or "closed-loop cognitive RSI" categories describe once triggered. For those two, nothing asks how RSI is reached — the actors doing the work are themselves post-RSI researchers, biological or artificial, working with post-RSI time and resources, and the question is retrospective and academic: now that we have RSI, what is the smallest thing that would have sufficed? A later section below deliberately steps outside this premise to ask a present-tense version instead, and says so explicitly where it does.

### The question

The document treats closed-loop RSI as gated by compute and infrastructure large enough for tripwires 1, 2, 3, and 6 to observe. It does not ask a prior question: what is the theoretical minimum configuration — hardware and software — capable of sustaining a self-improvement loop at all, independent of when current brute-force systems reach it?

This matters beyond curiosity. If the true minimum sits far below current designs, the document's physical-friction argument — atoms have cycle times, chips have a narrow chokepoint, compute thresholds are visible — may describe a door that looks far away only because nobody has gone looking for the small one.

### Two candidate routes, with different failure modes

**Ablation.** Start from a known-working RSI system and strip components until it breaks, then back off one step. Direct precedent: Venter's minimal-genome project deleted genes from a working cell until replication failed, arriving at JCVI-syn3.0 (473 genes) — smaller than any natural organism, larger than theoretical predictions, containing genes of then-unknown function that proved necessary.

Ablation has a known failure mode: **path dependence**, a *frozen accident* in Gould's term. An early, arbitrary design choice gets locked in because everything built afterward depends on it. Stripping finds *a* local minimum reachable via that specific deletion path — not necessarily the global minimum reachable from a different starting architecture. The genetic code itself is the canonical instance: near-universal, almost certainly not optimal, fixed too early for anything since to escape it. A brute-force RSI system's early choices could be exactly this kind of frozen accident, contaminating every downstream step in a way stripping alone cannot undo.

**Emergence through continued capability growth.** Let the already-closed RSI loop keep running — accumulating post-RSI cycles, each one sharpening the analytic capability that would eventually be turned on this very question — until that capability is sufficient that the minimal-RSI problem yields a much simpler answer as a byproduct. Two distinct mathematical precedents, worth separating: *re-proof* (a result that once needed extensive machinery later gets a dramatically simpler proof once the surrounding theory matures) and *outright resolution* (a genuinely open problem — a Riemann Hypothesis, a Tate conjecture — gets solved not by brute search but by a new concept making the answer visible for the first time). Minimal-RSI could fall out of either form. This route isn't path-dependent on any single ancestor's design choices the way ablation is, but offers no guarantee of happening at all, or on any bounded timescale.

Nothing about this mechanism actually requires the post-RSI premise above. Ordinary pre-RSI capability growth — the scaling and research already underway — could produce the same kind of unexpectedly simple reframing, by the same logic, before anyone deliberately builds the large brute-force system the premise assumed. That possibility is developed below as its own point, because unlike the retrospective post-RSI question, it bears on the present.

### A sharper, present-tense version: is anyone already looking, and could they already have it?

Route 2, read without the post-RSI restriction above, implies something more immediate than either route originally suggested: a minimal RSI-triggering configuration could in principle be found by ordinary, already-underway research — not necessarily by a deliberate search for "the minimum" (though someone could be running exactly that search right now, in which case finding it would be no accident at all), but potentially also as a byproduct of the same kind of reframing that periodically simplifies a hard mathematical result once the surrounding theory matures. Either way, nobody needs to be *trying to find this specific thing* for it to be found.

This is a classic low-probability, high-impact structure, and the impact side is worth stating precisely. Tripwires 6 (training compute of the largest frontier run), 12 (energy/interconnect as the binding constraint), and 15 (decentralized training bypassing the chokepoint) — and arguably 10 (open-weight diffusion) — are calibrated against a specific assumption: that closing the loop requires large, visible compute. Tripwires 1–3 track capability trends directly and would likely still register *some* signal from a minimal design, so they are not the ones this scenario would blind. The algorithmic-efficiency passage in the main document already tracks one way the compute assumption erodes — training gets cheaper for a fixed capability level, gradually, on a curve. This is not that. If a minimal design is found, it does not erode those three or four tripwires gradually; it invalidates their calibration at once, because the missing ingredient was never more compute but a different way of framing the problem — the same pattern as inventing schemes before certain conjectures in algebraic geometry became tractable, aimed at the present instead of at some future post-RSI theorist.

The uncomfortable part, worth stating as plainly as the main document's treacherous-turn note does for a different reason: **this is close to unfalsifiable until the moment it isn't.** There is no observable signature of "a small solution exists and hasn't been found yet" — by construction, the evidence a reader would want (a failed search, a proof of non-existence) doesn't exist either, since absence of a discovered solution is not evidence of no solution, only of no discovery so far. That cuts against treating this as a tripwire — there is nothing to threshold — and for the same reason it cuts against dismissing it. The honest position is that the main document's confidence in its own compute-calibrated tripwires rests on an unexamined assumption: that nobody doing ordinary research has already stumbled onto the small door. Nothing here argues that assumption is false. Nothing here can rule it out either.

### Why it resists a clean answer regardless of route

"Self-improvement" needs a rigorous, benchmark-independent definition before either route is well-posed — trivial definitions admit trivially small solutions. The underlying quantity is close to Kolmogorov complexity, the shortest program producing given behavior, which is uncomputable in general. Lower and upper bounds are provable in principle; an exact minimum may not be reachable by proof, only approached.

### What would help

Not a solution. A more rigorous formalization of "self-improvement loop"; known results in complexity theory, self-replicating automata (von Neumann's universal constructor, plausibly), or minimal-agent literature bearing on either route; whether ablation's path-dependence problem has a known mitigation in the minimal-genome literature that transfers here; or an argument that the question is ill-posed, which would itself be a useful answer.

## Is durable AI control a logical impossibility?

**Status:** open. This document rejects the strong form of the claim below but does not consider the question closed, for reasons stated at the end. Reviewed twice externally (Opus 5.5, then Grok against two primary interview transcripts, both Oct 2026); the second round found a fabricated quotation in the first and corrected it, along with several sourcing and argument fixes incorporated below and in the main document.

**Why it's here rather than in the main document:** this bears on the outcome table's basic shape — specifically, on whether the "alignment largely solved" rows should carry meaningful probability at all, and on how those rows are defined — and the argument deserves to be stated in its strongest form rather than compressed into a caveat.

### The claim, stated as its strongest proponent states it

Roman Yampolskiy — who has described himself as having coined the term "AI safety" (his own account gives 2010 in one bio and 2011 in another; treat the specific year as self-description, not an independently settled fact), directs the Cyber Security Lab at the University of Louisville, and has written extensively on the question, including informal argument alongside some borrowed formal results (*On Controllability of AI*; *Unpredictability of AI*; *Unexplainability and Incomprehensibility of AI*; the 2024 book *AI: Unexplainable, Unpredictable, Uncontrollable*) — holds that durable human control of a superintelligent system is not merely difficult but a logical impossibility.

His P(doom) figure is harder to pin to a single clean primary statement than most reporting on it suggests. In one interview, 99.9% is the interviewer's figure, offered as a question ("is that your number?"); Yampolskiy does not repeat it, answering instead via the impossibility framing — a perpetual-motion device gets probability zero, and that is how he wants the number read. In the Lex Fridman interview, "99.9" appears as his stated ceiling on formal-verification confidence ("we can get 99.9... but we never get to 100 percent"), not as an extinction probability. 99.9999% appears only in secondary reporting, not confirmed in either transcript checked against this entry. The figures most often quoted are not false, but they compress a more qualified set of statements than a single number suggests.

On horizon: rather than a clean "99.9% over 100 years" pairing, the clearest statement is his own, on timing specifically — "timing matters... yes, we all probably will die, but 80 years is not 2 years" — a direct rejection of treating near-term and long-term risk as the same claim. Separately, in the Lex Fridman interview, both the interviewer's framing and Yampolskiy's own language ("a hundred years or more") use a century-scale horizon for the zero-bug and decision-volume arguments specifically, without pairing that horizon to a specific extinction percentage.

**His actual mechanism is iterative, not a single verification question — and this document's first attempt to state it put words in his mouth that he did not say.** An earlier version of this entry presented a composite sentence in quotation marks as a direct quote; it was a reconstruction, not something he said in either of the two transcripts checked against it, and the quotation marks were wrong. Corrected below with real quotes, attributed to the specific interview each comes from, since the two differ in emphasis.

In the Lex Fridman Podcast #431 interview (lexfridman.com/roman-yampolskiy-transcript): "the problem of controlling AGI or superintelligence... is like a problem of creating a perpetual safety machine. By analogy with perpetual motion machine, it's impossible." Systems "keep improving, learning, eventually self-modifying, interacting with the environment, interacting with malevolent actors." Asked to compare this to ordinary cybersecurity: a password compromise gets "a new password"; this does not get a second chance — "what are the chances that we'll create the most complex software ever on the first try with zero bugs and it'll continue to have zero bugs for a hundred years or more."

In a separate interview: building general superintelligence means "we won't be able to control it," making the outcome "basically guaranteed" negative; control would require "a perpetual safety device," and "by analogy with perpetual motion machine, you can't do that" — no prototype exists, and no one has a working idea of how to control something smarter than all of humanity combined.

Four elements run through both: the system keeps changing, so each new version must be re-secured; the horizon is indefinite, not a fixed deployment window; something — the system itself, or an external adversary — actively optimizes against whatever safeguards exist; and a single failure is not recoverable the way a compromised password is.

He has also argued, separately, that the different *kinds* of control each fail in their own way: a system that obeys literally is dangerous because literal obedience doesn't track intent; a system that decides on humanity's behalf, however well, is no longer a system humans control. This is not a detail — it bears directly on the estimates finding below.

His own framing already extends the analogy to weaker, partial-control schemes without needing to be asked: a *perpetual safety device* — anything short of full, permanent control — is also impossible by the same reasoning, since any achieved level of safety degrades as the system keeps changing. This is not a fringe position dressed as one. It is specific, named, and argued at length by someone with real technical standing, and it deserves to be represented at that strength — and, as corrected above, represented in his actual words rather than a close paraphrase presented as one.

### Where this document's structure is actually in tension with it

Two places:

**First:** if Yampolskiy is right, the "alignment largely solved" rows are not conservative branches to weight modestly — they are closer to probability assigned to a working perpetual motion machine having been built.

**Second:** the tripwire framework's mechanism is observation of behavior under scrutiny. The main document already names the gap this creates: *"a system performing alignment convincingly under evaluation while holding a different disposition once monitoring lifts... predicts clean results from every tripwire here, by design; a genuinely aligned system looks identical."* Yampolskiy's objection reframes this named gap as the central case a sufficiently capable system produces by default, not a corner case the framework mostly avoids.

### Why this document does not adopt the strong conclusion

Not because the underlying theoretical results are wrong, and not primarily on the strength of an empirical-reliability analogy to aviation or nuclear power — on reflection, that analogy is weaker than it first appears, for reasons given below. The sharper rebuttal is more precise than "no proof isn't disqualifying": **Rice's theorem blocks deciding a given property for *all* programs — it does not block building one specific program and proving that it has a given property.** Verified systems exist and are not hypothetical: seL4 (a formally verified microkernel) and CompCert (a formally verified C compiler) are real, deployed instances of exactly this distinction.

**Yampolskiy's actual objection is sharper than the general Rice's-theorem argument this rebuttal first addresses, and seL4 does not answer it.** Three specific moves, each in his own words, each independent of the general unverifiability claim:

First, a verifier regress: a verifier for a system of equal or greater complexity than itself is a catch-22, since confirming the next system's safety would require an already-proven-safe system at least as capable to do the confirming. Second, residual error compounding under volume: even a high verification-confidence ceiling ("99.9... but we never get to 100 percent") is not enough headroom if the system makes a billion decisions a second across a century — the error budget is exhausted by throughput, not by the confidence figure alone. Third, and most direct: **asked whether building AI by design rather than by evolution — the approach seL4-style verification represents — could chart a path around this, he grants the path exists in principle, and rejects it on empirical grounds: it was already tried, for the first fifty years of the field, and it does not scale to produce competitive capability.** This is not a caution the document is adding on his behalf — it is his own stated objection to roughly the move seL4 represents.

The honest position, then, is narrower than either side's strongest framing. Formal impossibility-in-general does not entail impossibility-for-a-specific-well-constructed-system — seL4 and CompCert are real proof of that much. But Yampolskiy's actual case against applying that lesson to AI does not rest on the general claim this rebuttal answers; it rests on verifier regress, error compounding under volume, and an empirical claim that provably-constructed systems historically fail to produce competitive intelligence. Frontier models are trained, not built to a verification spec, which sits them closer to Rice's "arbitrary program" case than to seL4 — and his specific objection says that gap is not an engineering oversight correctable by building the next one more carefully, but the reason the capable systems and the provable ones have not yet been the same systems.

The aviation and nuclear analogy, on examination, fails on exactly the four features in Yampolskiy's own mechanism above: aviation software is fixed and certified version by version, not continuously self-modifying; physics does not optimize against an interlock the way an adversary or the system itself might; aviation became safe largely by learning from *survivable* crashes, which a civilization-scale failure is not; and testing happens in the regime the system will actually operate in, which is specifically unavailable for a system more capable than anything tested before. It is kept here only as a weaker, supplementary point — formal completeness is not the normal bar any safety-critical field meets — not as the primary rebuttal.

**The real crux, stated plainly rather than left implicit: how likely is it that a capable system strategically deceives evaluators by default, rather than as a rare failure mode?** That is an empirical question with a probability attached to it, not a theorem with a binary answer. Yampolskiy's 99.9% figure requires this probability to be close to certain. This document does not know the answer and states that directly rather than resolving it by analogy.

**One further, narrower methodological concern, and it cuts in both directions.** "Every time I meet an independent concern, I have to add more nines to my estimate, as if I wasn't already close to one" is a real, pinned statement of his method, consistent across separate interviews — he also describes the same effect as fractal: "the more you zoom in, the more problems you see." Stacking probabilities multiplicatively toward certainty is only valid if the worries are independent. If several share a common root — the same underlying verification difficulty recurring in different guises — naive stacking overstates the tail. This does not by itself produce a lower number: under full correlation, the doom probability collapses to the root cause's own probability, and if that root is itself near-certain, the stacking critique doesn't move the figure at all. It relocates the disagreement to the root's probability rather than resolving it.

More useful than the critique alone is that it applies symmetrically. Yampolskiy's own strongest quantitative argument is per-iteration compounding: if each version is safe with independent probability *p*, survival across *N* iterations is *p^N*, which goes to zero as *N* grows. That argument assumes independence across iterations in exactly the way the stacking critique challenges. If safety is instead a property that persists once achieved — if solving it once makes solving it again more likely rather than each iteration being a fresh independent roll — long-run survival could sit far above *p^N*. The same analytical tool that challenges an over-confident low doom estimate challenges an over-confident high one; applied consistently, it does not favor either conclusion on its own.

### What this means for the estimates — two findings, not a general objection

**First, the "alignment largely solved" condition was ambiguous between two different readings, and the ambiguity was real, not merely a matter of phrasing.** The document defines flourishing as requiring humans "retain meaningful collective agency," and disempowerment as humans having "lost effective control over civilizational direction" — independent of whether the outcome is good. A value-aligned superintelligence that acts beneficently without deferring to human judgment fits the disempowerment definition exactly, regardless of outcome quality, which is precisely the control-type failure described above. The main document now states explicitly that "alignment largely solved," as the outcome table uses the term, requires the agency-preserving sense — not good outcomes alone. This resolves the ambiguity by definition rather than by moving probability mass, which is the right fix if (and only if) that was the sense intended when the original figures were set. A reader who takes "solved" to mean only "good outcomes, deference or not" should read the flourishing figures in those two rows as overstated and the disempowerment figures as correspondingly understated — a legitimate different reading of the same table under a different definition, not an error in it.

**Second, the single largest lever in the table is structurally unobservable, and the document now says so directly.** Solving alignment moves disempowerment by roughly 31 points — the biggest swing any single factor produces. But no tripwire distinguishes genuine alignment from deceptive alignment that is behaviorally identical under evaluation, and `P(alignment largely solved | RSI closes)` is never stated as a number anywhere in the document. This is not a miscalibrated cell; it is a missing parameter plus a structural limit on what the tripwire apparatus can ever revise, now stated explicitly in the main text rather than left implicit.

**Third, the table does not state which of two different claims "alignment largely solved" is making, and Yampolskiy's own timing distinction — "80 years is not 2 years" — is the reason this matters.** His load-bearing argument is about *persistence under continued self-modification*: each new version is a fresh zero-bug requirement, and solving alignment once does not mean it stays solved through the next hundred self-modifications. The table scores "largely solved" as a state reached at loop-close and then holds the flourishing figures through 2060. That is consistent with two different readings — "solved, and holds for the 2040–2060 assessment window specifically" (a bounded claim) or "solved, in the stronger sense of remaining solved indefinitely under continued modification" (the claim his argument actually targets) — and the document does not say which one it means. This is a reason to state the distinction explicitly, not yet a reason to move the cells: a missing `P(\text{remains solved across further modification} \mid \text{solved once})` is a gap in the model, not a demonstrated error in any specific figure.

No other specific cell has a demonstrated case for being miscalibrated.

### What remains genuinely unresolved

The treacherous-turn gap is real and this entry does not close it. No tripwire here would catch a system executing Yampolskiy's predicted failure mode correctly. The disagreement in this document is narrower than "he is wrong": *absence of a thermodynamics-grade impossibility proof* does not license *near-certainty of failure*, and a specific, well-constructed system can in principle have a property that no general procedure can verify for arbitrary systems — but current frontier models are not built the way formally verified systems are built, and the document does not know, and says it does not know, how likely strategic deception by default actually is. A reader who takes formal unverifiability as sufficient grounds for near-certain doom should, on that premise, discount the "alignment largely solved" rows heavily — a legitimate reading of this table, not a different document.

### What would help

Evidence bearing on the actual crux — the probability that a capable system deceives evaluators by default rather than as a rare failure — from either direction; a response to his specific claim that provably-constructed, design-rather-than-evolution AI was already tried and does not scale to competitive capability, since that is the move this entry's own rebuttal most needs to survive; and a stated value, or stated range, for `P(remains solved across continued self-modification | solved once)`, which is the missing parameter the third estimates finding above identifies.
